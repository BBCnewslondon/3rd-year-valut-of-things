
Gravitational waves were first predicted in 1916 by Albert Einstein. They can be described as oscillations in the fabric of spacetime. These waves propagate at the speed of light.[1]![[Pasted image 20251030140900.png]]*Figure 1: The evolution of the different multipole terms of the gravitational potential for a simulated binary system. While the monopole (mass) and dipole (momentum) terms are constant or zero in the center-of-mass frame, the quadrupole term oscillates. This time-varying quadrupole moment is what sources the emission of gravitational waves, carrying energy away from the system.[10] (Image credit: Armaan Sachdeva)* 

Gravitational waves are formed in a similar manner to electromagnetic waves. However the key difference is that mass is subject to conservation laws of momentum. As a result the gravitational monopole and dipole cannot emit any sort of radiation. With all this in mind the lowest order and dominant form of Gravitational wave emission would be quadrupolar. The strain $h$ would be proportional to the 2nd time derivative $\frac{\partial^2}{\partial t^2}$ of the systems quadrupole moment$(I_{i,j})$ . Where $$h\sim\frac{G}{c^4 r} \ddot{I}_{ij}$$ The $\frac{1}{c^4}$ term in the relationship really shows how weak gravity is. This is the reason why enormous astronomical objects are needed which have asymmetrically  accelerating mass distributions like a binary star system which is spiraling inwards or objects like black holes or neutron stars. For these systems the quadrupole moment will vary periodically as the masses orbit (Figure 1) , radiating the orbital energy away as gravitational waves and leading to the system to eventually death spiral and eventually merge.[8]

# Detectors

The detection of gravitational wave strain is predicted to be $h\sim 10^{-21}$ for events such as the events previously stated. In order to measure this we are essentially measuring a displacement which is less than 0.001% the width of a proton over a distance of 4km. Detectors like LIGO, Virgo and KAGRA use power recycled, Fabry-Perot Michelson interferometers[2]. These work by splitting laser light and sending it down particular arms, where it is then made to resonate within Fabry-Perot cavities, which increases the light's travel time before it recombines by a factor of 10. As a gravitational wave passes through it will strain the arms slightly, causing a path difference between the arms. This path difference causes a constructive interference at a detector which can be measured[6].

The main challenge in detection is the immense precision required. The mirrors are hung from a quad pendulum to reduce background vibration, and this pendulum itself is connected to hydraulics with an automatic correction system to counteract any external vibrations. The laser itself also needs to be incredibly precise in both power and wavelength, as the laser is acting as a 'ruler'[9].

# Observations
The first discovery of a binary pulsar (1974)[7] was described as a naturally occurring relativity laboratory due to their huge gravitational fields. The binary’s orbital period showed a long-term, non-periodic decrease, establishing
strong evidence of gravitational radiation.

The first direct measurement of a gravitational wave was caused by a stellar mass black hole binary merger (2015). The waveform was consistent with Einstein's predictions from general relativity. Both LIGO detectors picked up a transient signal with a signal-to-noise ratio of 24.

A binary neutron star merger was observed later (2017) jointly by the LIGO and VIRGO detector. The near simultaneous gamma ray burst and gravitational radiation signal proved that gravitational waves propagate at the speed of light. This event also lead to evidence proving that binary neutron star mergers are sites of r-process nucleosynthesis, an event responsible for the formation of some heavier metals in the universe like gold and platinum. Furthermore, the detected gravitational waves and electromagnetic radiation allowed us to determine the distance and red shift of the galaxy, respectively.

Amongst the three combinations of merger events, detections can lie within the upper mass gap; a region where within binaries, objects are more massive than the largest mass neutron stars but less massive than the lowest mass black holes. A possible explanation for these peculiar measurements is that of lensing within a BH-NS merger.[11]
